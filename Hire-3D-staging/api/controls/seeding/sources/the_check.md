**FWA ENFORCEMENT CONTEXT — EFFECTIVE 7 APRIL 2026**
The Fair Work Agency launches 7 April 2026 with enforcement powers across NMW, SSP, holiday pay, right-to-work, and labour supply chains. It can conduct unannounced inspections, bring tribunal claims directly on behalf of workers, recover its own investigation costs from non-compliant employers, and refer cases for criminal prosecution. Every control in THE CHECK maps directly to one or more FWA enforcement areas. These are not theoretical risks for small businesses — the FWA has been explicitly designed to pursue the SME market that HMRC and previous enforcement bodies under-resourced.
## **1 — Employee Contracts and Written Particulars**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether every PAYE employee has a written contract or statement of written particulars that was issued on or before their first day, and whether those documents reflect the actual current working arrangements.
**Why This Matters:**
Written particulars are a day-one legal right under the Employment Rights Act 1996 as amended. Failure to provide them is a standalone breach that the Fair Work Agency can enforce directly from April 2026. In any tribunal claim — unfair dismissal, unlawful deduction, discrimination — the absence of a written contract removes the employer’s ability to rely on agreed terms. The tribunal will imply terms in the employee’s favour. From January 2027, anyone employed from July 2026 onwards will have unfair dismissal rights from day one, with compensation that is now uncapped under the Employment Rights Act 2025. A founder who has never issued a written contract to even one employee is exposed from the moment that person raises a grievance or resigns under notice. This is the most basic and most frequently missing document in small businesses.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me a signed written contract or statement of particulars for every current employee — and can you confirm these were issued on or before their first day? If anything has changed since they started — pay, hours, role — can you show me how that was documented?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me the contracts — one for each current employee.
• Are all of them signed — by both sides?
• What date was each contract issued relative to their start date?
• Has anything changed since the contract was signed — pay rise, role change, hours change? Show me how that was recorded.
• Is there anyone working for you on a PAYE basis who does not have a written contract?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A signed written contract or statement of particulars for every PAYE employee
• Issued on or before day one — not weeks or months after starting
• Contents reflect reality — actual pay, actual hours, actual job title
• Any changes since original issue documented in writing — a letter, email confirmation, or signed addendum
**Sampling:**
All current PAYE employees — this is a full population check, not a sample.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Contracts exist for most but not all employees — gaps with no explanation
• Contracts issued but not signed by the employee
• Contracts issued weeks or months after the employee started
• Pay, hours, or role has changed but the contract has never been updated
• Anyone working on a regular PAYE basis with no written documentation at all
## **2 — Contractor and Freelancer Agreements**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether every contractor or freelancer doing regular work for the business has a written agreement — and whether that agreement covers the essentials: scope, payment, intellectual property ownership, confidentiality, and termination.
**Why This Matters:**
Without a written agreement, anything a contractor creates — code, designs, copy, processes — legally belongs to them, not the business. The Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 vests ownership in the creator unless there is a written assignment. For a business that relies on contractor-produced work, this is an existential risk that surfaces immediately in any investment, sale, or dispute. Beyond IP, an undocumented contractor relationship with no written terms creates ambiguity on payment, notice, and obligations — and if the relationship looks like employment, the business faces retrospective PAYE and NI liability that HMRC and from April 2026 the Fair Work Agency can pursue for up to six years. A purchase order or email chain is not a written agreement.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me the written agreements you have with contractors and freelancers — and can you confirm they cover who owns the work, what the confidentiality obligations are, and how the arrangement ends?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me the agreements — one for each regular contractor or freelancer.
• Does each agreement contain an IP assignment clause — confirming the business owns what they produce?
• Is there a confidentiality or non-disclosure clause?
• How does the arrangement terminate — is there a notice period or project end clause?
• Are there any contractors working regularly without any written agreement at all?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A written agreement for every contractor or freelancer doing regular or significant work
• An explicit IP assignment clause — not implied, written
• A confidentiality clause covering client data, business information, and commercially sensitive material
• Termination terms — how the arrangement ends and what happens to work in progress
**Sampling:**
All current regular contractors and freelancers — full population.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Agreements exist for some but not all contractors
• Agreements exist but contain no IP assignment clause — contractor retains ownership
• Work being done on email exchange or purchase order only with no formal agreement
• Old agreements that no longer reflect the current scope or relationship
• No confidentiality clause in any contractor agreement
## **3 — IR35 and Contractor Employment Status**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has considered whether any of its contractors might be considered employed for tax purposes — and whether the working arrangements in practice support the self-employed status being claimed.
**Why This Matters:**
IR35 liability in the small business sector (below the off-payroll rules threshold) sits primarily with the contractor — but that does not make the business immune. If HMRC determines that a contractor relationship is disguised employment, the business may face retrospective PAYE and NI contributions, interest, and penalties going back up to six years. From April 2026, the Fair Work Agency has concurrent enforcement powers in this area and can pursue labour supply chain non-compliance proactively. The risk indicators are well established: exclusive or near-exclusive work for one client, set hours controlled by the client, use of the client’s equipment, no genuine right of substitution, a relationship that has run for years without review. Many small business founders have contractors who tick all of these boxes and have never considered the implications.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you walk me through your contractor arrangements — specifically whether any of them work only or mainly for you, whether you control their hours, and whether they use your equipment? Have you ever looked at whether any of them might be considered employed for tax purposes?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• How many contractors do you currently use — and how long have each of them been working with you?
• Does any contractor work exclusively or almost exclusively for your business?
• Do you control when and how they work — set hours, specific methods, supervision?
• Do they use your equipment, systems, or premises to do the work?
• Do they send invoices and have their own insurance and business infrastructure?
• Have you or they ever run an HMRC CEST assessment — can you show me the result?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Awareness of IR35 risk and which contractors might be borderline
• Working arrangements that are genuinely consistent with self-employment — multiple clients, own equipment, genuine substitution right
• For any borderline cases — a documented assessment, ideally a CEST result, showing the determination was considered
• Contractor relationships reviewed periodically — not set up once and never revisited
**Sampling:**
All current contractors — discuss each one. Focus on longest-running and most integrated arrangements.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Contractor works exclusively for the business with no other clients
• Business controls hours, location, and method of work
• Contractor uses business equipment and systems
• Relationship has run for 18 months or more with no review
• No awareness of IR35 at all — founder has never considered it
## **4 — Right-to-Work Checks and Evidence Retention**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether a compliant right-to-work check was conducted for every employee before or on their first day, whether the evidence was retained correctly, and whether follow-up checks are in place for anyone with time-limited permission to work.
**Why This Matters:**
Right-to-work civil penalties are £45,000 per illegal worker for a first breach and £60,000 per worker for a repeat breach — in force from 13 February 2024. These are strict liability penalties: the Home Office does not require intent, only that the prescribed check was not correctly completed and documented. There is no insurance product that covers these fines. The Fair Work Agency takes on proactive right-to-work enforcement from 7 April 2026 with powers to conduct unannounced inspections. A business with three employees where one check was never properly completed and retained faces a £45,000 fine on first inspection. Criminal prosecution for knowingly employing someone without the right to work carries unlimited fines and up to five years’ imprisonment. For small founders who have never been through this before, the most common failure is not deliberate — it is simply not knowing what a compliant check looks like or not keeping the evidence.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me right-to-work evidence for every current employee — the actual documents you checked, retained as copies, stored somewhere you can access them? And if anyone has a visa or time-limited permission, can you show me the follow-up check system?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me where you store right-to-work evidence — can you retrieve it now?
• For each employee — what document did you check, when was it checked, and is the copy here?
• Was the check done before or on day one — or after they started?
• Does anyone in the team have a visa, work permit, or time-limited right to work? If so, when does it expire and when is the follow-up check due?
• Who conducted the checks — was it always the founder, or has someone else done them?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A right-to-work check completed for every current employee — on or before day one
• Evidence retained — a copy of the document checked, stored securely and retrievable
• Evidence that meets Home Office requirements — correct document type, copy certified, dated
• Time-limited permissions tracked — expiry dates known and follow-up checks scheduled
**Sampling:**
Full population — every current employee. This cannot be sampled.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Check done but evidence not retained — “I checked it at the time but didn’t keep a copy”
• Check done after the employee started work — no statutory excuse
• Photocopies taken by a recruiter or agency relied upon without the employer independently verifying
• Time-limited permissions not tracked — expiry date unknown
• No check conducted at all for one or more employees
## **5 — Payroll, NMW, SSP, and Pension Auto-Enrolment**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether PAYE is correctly operated, National Minimum Wage is being paid to all workers, Statutory Sick Pay is being administered correctly from day one, holiday pay is calculated on the right basis, and workplace pension auto-enrolment obligations are being met.
**Why This Matters:**
This single control covers the four highest-priority Fair Work Agency enforcement areas from April 2026. National Minimum Wage: the FWA enforces NMW with a penalty of 200% of the underpayment per worker, capped at £20,000 per worker, plus public naming on the government’s naming and shaming register. Underpayment is more common than founders realise — salaried workers doing long hours, workers making deductions from pay for uniforms or equipment, unpaid induction time. Statutory Sick Pay: from 6 April 2026, SSP is payable from day one of sickness (the three waiting days are abolished), the lower earnings limit is removed (all workers qualify regardless of earnings), and the FWA enforces SSP with the same 200% penalty regime as NMW. Holiday pay: the FWA can recover up to six years of underpaid holiday pay on behalf of workers — and from April 2026, failure to keep holiday pay records is a standalone criminal offence. Holiday pay must include regular overtime and commission, not just basic salary. Pensions: The Pensions Regulator issues fixed penalty notices of £400 for auto-enrolment failures, escalating to £50–£10,000 per day. Every worker earning over £10,000 aged 22 to state pension age must be enrolled. Small founders frequently miss this entirely in the first year of employment.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you walk me through how payroll works — who runs it, how NMW compliance is checked, how sick pay is handled, how holiday pay is calculated, and whether your pension auto-enrolment is set up and running?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Who runs payroll — you, your accountant, a payroll provider? How often does it run?
• What is the lowest hourly rate anyone in the business is paid — can you confirm it is above the current NMW for their age group?
• If an employee was off sick last month — how was SSP calculated and when was it paid from?
• How is holiday pay calculated — just basic salary, or does it include regular overtime and commission?
• Have you set up a workplace pension scheme — show me the provider confirmation and the contribution records.
• Is anyone not enrolled who should be — have you checked eligibility for all workers?
• How far back do your payroll and holiday records go — can you retrieve records from three years ago?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• PAYE registered and operating correctly — RTI submissions current, payslips issued every pay period
• NMW compliance demonstrated — lowest paid worker’s effective hourly rate calculated and above the current rate
• SSP administered from day one of sickness with no waiting days applied from 6 April 2026
• Holiday pay calculated correctly — including regular overtime and commission, not basic salary only
• Payroll and holiday records retained for a minimum of six years
• Auto-enrolment pension in place — all eligible workers enrolled, contributions deducted and paid to provider each month
**Sampling:**
Last three payroll runs. Most recent sick absence if available. Holiday pay calculation for one variable-pay worker if applicable. Pension contribution records.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• NMW compliance assumed but never calculated — particularly risk for salaried workers doing long hours
• SSP still being paid with three waiting days applied after 6 April 2026
• Holiday pay calculated on basic salary only — overtime and commission excluded
• Payroll records not retained beyond current year
• Auto-enrolment not set up despite having eligible workers
• Pension contributions deducted from employees but not paid to the provider — a criminal offence
## **6 — Core Employment Policies**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has the minimum written policies it is legally required to have — and whether those policies are accessible to staff and reflect current law.
**Why This Matters:**
A disciplinary and grievance procedure is not optional — it is a statutory requirement, and the ACAS Code of Practice on Disciplinary and Grievance Procedures is legally binding in tribunal proceedings. Failure to follow the Code allows a tribunal to uplift any award by up to 25%. From January 2027, unfair dismissal rights apply from day one for anyone hired from July 2026, with compensation now uncapped under the Employment Rights Act 2025 — meaning a poorly handled dismissal of even a short-tenure employee can result in a six-figure tribunal award. The Fair Work Agency can enforce failures in absence and sickness policies from April 2026. A health and safety policy is a legal requirement for businesses with five or more employees under the Health and Safety at Work Act 1974. An equality policy is not strictly mandatory but its absence is treated as an aggravating factor in discrimination claims. Policies downloaded from the internet and never customised or communicated are, in practice, the same as no policies — a tribunal will ask whether the policy was actually known and followed.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me your disciplinary and grievance procedure, your equality policy, and your health and safety policy — and can you tell me whether staff know these exist and where to find them?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me your disciplinary and grievance procedure — where is it written down?
• Does it follow the ACAS Code of Practice minimum stages — investigation, hearing, outcome, right of appeal?
• Do staff know it exists — has it been communicated, and can an employee find it without asking you?
• When was it last reviewed — does it reflect the Employment Rights Act 2025 changes?
• Do you have a written equality or anti-discrimination policy?
• If you have five or more employees — do you have a written health and safety policy?
• Is there an absence or sickness policy — does it reflect the new day-one SSP entitlement?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A written disciplinary and grievance procedure that follows the ACAS Code of Practice minimum stages
• Staff aware of the procedure and able to access it
• A written equality or anti-discrimination policy
• A written health and safety policy if the business has five or more employees
• An absence/sickness policy reflecting day-one SSP entitlement from 6 April 2026
• Policies reviewed within the last 12 months — not pre-2024 downloads
**Sampling:**
Document review of all policies. Ask one member of staff whether they know where the grievance procedure is.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Disciplinary procedure exists but has never been communicated to staff
• Procedure does not include a right of appeal — non-compliant with ACAS Code
• Policies downloaded from the internet and never customised to the business
• No health and safety policy despite having five or more employees
• Absence policy still references three SSP waiting days after 6 April 2026 — out of date and non-compliant
• No policies at all — everything handled informally
## **7 — Data Protection, Confidentiality, and ICO Registration**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business is handling employee and contractor personal data in a way that meets its basic GDPR obligations — and whether confidentiality obligations are documented in contracts.
**Why This Matters:**
Every business that processes personal data — which means every business with even one employee — is subject to UK GDPR. The ICO can fine up to £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover for serious breaches. For small businesses, the more common risk is not a catastrophic fine but the combination of a Subject Access Request they cannot fulfil, a data breach they do not report within 72 hours, or a former employee who takes client data on departure because there was no confidentiality clause. From April 2026, the Fair Work Agency will have access to employer records including payroll data — making data handling practices part of any FWA inspection. ICO registration costs £40–£60 per year and is a legal requirement for most businesses that process personal data. Failure to register is a criminal offence.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me how you store employee and contractor personal data — whether it is secure and access-controlled — and can you confirm that your employment and contractor contracts include confidentiality clauses?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Where is personal data about staff stored — HR system, shared drive, spreadsheet, email? Who has access?
• If an employee made a Subject Access Request today — could you compile and send their personal data within 30 days?
• If a data breach occurred today — would you know to report it to the ICO within 72 hours, and do you know how?
• Do employment contracts include a confidentiality clause?
• Do contractor agreements include a confidentiality clause?
• Are you registered with the ICO — can you show me the registration?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Personal data stored securely with access controlled to those who need it
• Awareness of Subject Access Request obligations and a basic process for responding
• Awareness of the 72-hour breach reporting obligation and a basic process for identifying and reporting breaches
• Confidentiality clauses in all employment contracts and contractor agreements
• ICO registration in place and current
**Sampling:**
Data storage review — where is it, who can access it. Contract confidentiality clause check. ICO registration check.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Staff personal data in open-access spreadsheets or shared drives with no access controls
• Personal data stored in personal email accounts or on personal devices with no controls
• No awareness of Subject Access Request or breach reporting obligations
• No confidentiality clause in employment contracts or contractor agreements
• Not registered with the ICO despite being legally required to
## **8 — Record-Keeping and Document Retrieval**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business can locate and produce its core employment documents quickly — and whether someone other than the founder knows where they are.
**Why This Matters:**
The Fair Work Agency from 7 April 2026 can conduct unannounced inspections and require immediate production of employment records including contracts, right-to-work evidence, payroll records, and holiday records. Failure to produce records is treated as evidence of non-compliance and can result in the FWA issuing enforcement notices and recovering its investigation costs directly from the employer. In employment tribunal proceedings, failure to produce relevant documents results in adverse inferences — the tribunal assumes the missing document would have supported the claimant. Under the Employment Rights Act 2025, failure to maintain holiday records for six years is now a standalone criminal offence. For small founders who store everything in their own email or on their laptop, the practical question is not whether the documents exist — it is whether they can be found and produced in the time an inspection or claim requires.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_If I asked you right now to produce the employment contract, right-to-work evidence, and last three payslips for a specific employee — how long would that take, and where would you find them?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me where employment documents are stored — walk me through the system.
• Can you retrieve a specific document now — demonstrate it.
• Is there one place where all employment records live, or are they spread across email, drives, and filing cabinets?
• If you were unavailable — could anyone else find these documents without asking you?
• How far back do your records go — do you have records for people who left two or three years ago?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Core employment documents — contracts, RTW evidence, payroll records, holiday records — stored in one place
• Retrievable within minutes, not hours
• Someone other than the founder knows where they are and can access them
• Records retained for at least six years — including for leavers
**Sampling:**
Live retrieval test — ask for a specific document and time how long it takes.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Documents exist but are spread across email threads, multiple drives, and physical filing with no system
• Founder is the only person who knows where anything is
• Documents cannot be retrieved quickly — would take hours or days to compile for an inspection
• Records for leavers deleted or lost — nothing retained beyond current employees
• Holiday records not retained — criminal offence from April 2026
## **9 — Operational Resilience and Single Points of Failure**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business would keep functioning if the founder or a key person was suddenly unavailable — and whether at least one other person has access to the systems and information needed to keep basic operations running.
**Why This Matters:**
This control is not about formal business continuity planning — it is about basic operational resilience at the level every small business should have. For businesses with employees, the stakes are higher than for a sole trader: if the founder is hospitalised and no one can access payroll, employees do not get paid. That is an unlawful deduction from wages claim and, depending on severity and duration, a constructive dismissal risk. If no one can access banking, supplier payments stop and client deliveries fail. If key client relationships are held entirely in one person’s head with no CRM record or contact history, those relationships are at risk the moment that person is unreachable. The Fair Work Agency inspection regime makes this more acute: if an FWA inspector arrives and the person who holds all the employment records is unavailable, the business cannot demonstrate compliance in real time.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_If you were completely unreachable from tomorrow — who could keep payroll running, access the bank, deal with a staff issue, and find an employment document if an inspector arrived?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Who else has access to your payroll system or can authorise payroll in your absence?
• Who else has access to business banking — can they authorise payments?
• If an urgent staff issue arose — a grievance, an absence, an accident — who could handle it without you?
• Are key client relationships, supplier contacts, and critical business information stored somewhere others can access — or are they in your inbox and your memory?
• Is there a password manager or shared credentials system so critical systems can be accessed by more than one person?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• At least one other person with access to payroll and banking
• Key employment documents accessible to someone other than the founder
• Critical contact information and client relationships recorded somewhere shared — not only in personal email
• Basic awareness of who handles what if the founder is unavailable
**Sampling:**
Conversation only — no documents to review. Assess awareness and practical resilience.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Founder is sole signatory on banking with no deputy or second signatory
• No one else can run or authorise payroll
• Employment records accessible only via founder’s personal login or device
• All key relationships and critical information held personally with no shared record
• Founder genuinely unsure who would handle anything if they were unavailable
## **10 — People Risk Awareness**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the founder has a realistic and honest view of where their people-related risks currently sit — including key-person dependencies, unresolved issues, and known gaps they have not yet addressed.
**Why This Matters:**
This is the most important control in THE CHECK, and the most underestimated. People risk in small businesses is almost always visible to the founder before it becomes a crisis — the flight-risk employee who has gone quiet, the contractor arrangement that feels uncomfortable, the grievance that has been sidestepped rather than addressed, the person who holds all the technical knowledge and has started looking at LinkedIn. The Fair Work Agency from April 2026 brings a new dimension: workers who previously had no enforcement route for wage underpayment or holiday pay short-changing now have a government agency that will pursue their claim on their behalf, at no cost to them. A founder who has been underpaying overtime, miscalculating holiday pay, or informally managing someone out without process is now far more exposed than they were six months ago. This control is not about having perfect answers — it is about whether the founder knows where they are exposed and is thinking clearly about it.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_I want to ask you some honest questions about your team — not to catch you out, but because the founders who are clearest about where their risks sit are the ones who can do something about it. Is there anyone whose departure would cause you a serious problem? Any situation you know isn’t quite right that you haven’t addressed yet? Anything about your current setup that you already know needs fixing?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Is there anyone in the business whose departure would cause you a real problem — because of what they know, the relationships they hold, or the work only they can do?
• Are there any current people issues — performance concerns, unhappiness, a grievance that was raised informally and never properly resolved?
• Is there anything about your employment or contractor arrangements that you already know isn’t quite right — contracts not issued, IR35 risk not addressed, holiday pay not calculated correctly?
• Has the Fair Work Agency launch changed how you think about any of this — are there areas where you now feel more exposed?
• If I told you the FWA was conducting an inspection of your business next month — what would you most want to fix before they arrived?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Founder has a clear and honest view of where people risks currently sit
• Known issues acknowledged rather than dismissed
• At least a basic plan for addressing the most significant risk identified
• Awareness of how the FWA launch changes their exposure in specific areas
**Sampling:**
Conversation only — listen for what is said and what is not said. The quality of the answers here is the best predictor of overall compliance health.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Founder says everything is fine with no specific awareness of any risk — usually means they have not thought about it
• Known issues being actively avoided rather than addressed
• Contractor arrangements the founder knows are borderline but has not reviewed
• Underpayment or holiday pay issues the founder suspects but has not calculated
• A flight-risk employee whose departure would cause genuine operational disruption
**OPERATOR NOTE**
THE CHECK is delivered as a conversation, not an inspection. The tone throughout should be helpful and practical — the goal is to give the founder a clear picture of where they stand, what the real risks are, and what to do first. Every finding should be accompanied by a clear explanation of why it matters and what fixing it would involve. The FWA context is not used to frighten — it is used to help founders understand that the enforcement landscape has changed and that addressing these issues now is significantly cheaper than addressing them after an inspection or a tribunal claim.
