# **SECTION A — EMPLOYMENT & LEGAL EXPOSURE**
_Controls A1–A10 | 10 controls_
## **A1 — Contract Enforceability and Recorded Variations**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether written employment terms exist for all workers before they start, and whether any subsequent changes to those terms are formally documented.
**Why This Matters:**
Unsigned or missing contracts make notice periods, restrictive covenants, and salary terms unenforceable. Verbal variations create disputed liability. An investor or lender cannot quantify workforce obligation without knowing the contractual position of every worker.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me written contracts or offer letters for everyone currently working here — employees and contractors? And if anyone has had a pay rise, role change, or hours change since they started, can you show me how that was documented?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• How many people currently work here — employees and contractors combined?
• Show me contracts for three people — how quickly can you retrieve them?
• Are any contracts unsigned? How long have they been outstanding?
• When terms change, how is that documented — show me a recent example.
• Where are contracts stored and who controls access?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Signed contracts or accepted offer letters in place for 100% of workforce before work commenced
• Any recent unsigned contracts have a clear reason and are being actively progressed
• All subsequent variations documented in writing — letters, signed addenda, or confirmed email trails
• Contracts stored accessibly and retrievable on request
**Sampling:**
Stratified sample: two employees, two contractors, one senior or founder-level appointment.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Contracts produced for most but not all workers with no explanation for gaps
• Contracts signed weeks or months after start date with no documented reason
• Pay rises or role changes acknowledged verbally but no written record exists
• Contracts stored inconsistently or cannot be located without significant effort
## **A2 — Contractor Status Substantiation and IR35 Compliance**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has made and documented a reasonable employment status determination for every contractor, and whether working arrangements in practice reflect the status claimed.
**Why This Matters:**
IR35 misclassification is one of the most significant off-balance-sheet liabilities a growth business can carry. HMRC can pursue unpaid PAYE and NI contributions for up to six years, with interest and penalties. An investor acquiring or lending into a business with endemic misclassification inherits that liability on completion. The question is not whether contractors exist — it is whether their status is defensible.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_For every contractor or person paid via a limited company or umbrella arrangement, can you show me the employment status determination you made — and can you demonstrate that how they actually work reflects that determination?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me your contractor list — how many are there and what status determination was made for each?
• Walk me through how you made the status determination — did you use HMRC’s CEST tool, take advice, or document your reasoning?
• For a specific contractor — do they use their own equipment, set their own hours, and work for other clients?
• Has any contractor’s working arrangement changed since the original determination was made?
• Have you received any HMRC correspondence or enquiries about contractor status?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A documented status determination for every contractor — ideally a completed CEST record or written assessment
• Working arrangements in practice consistent with the status determined
• Regular review of contractor arrangements where working patterns evolve
• No reliance on contractor self-certification without independent verification
**Sampling:**
All contractors paid via PSC or umbrella arrangements. Sample two for working practice consistency.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Status determinations exist on paper but working arrangements suggest an employment relationship
• No documented determination — status assumed based on how the contractor invoices
• Contractors working exclusively for the business, using its equipment, with set hours — but classified as outside IR35
• No review since original engagement despite material changes in working pattern
## **A3 — Right-to-Work Verification and Secure Storage**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has conducted compliant right-to-work checks for all workers before employment commenced, and whether evidence is stored securely and follow-up checks are in place for time-limited permissions.
**Why This Matters:**
Right-to-work civil penalties are £45,000 per illegal worker for a first breach and £60,000 per worker for repeat breaches, in force from 13 February 2024. These are not recoverable through insurance. The Fair Work Agency, launching 7 April 2026, has proactive inspection powers and can visit without notice. A business with even a small number of unchecked workers carries material and immediate financial exposure.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me your right-to-work verification records for your current workforce — how the checks were conducted, what documents were seen, and where the evidence is stored?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me your RTW register or tracker — does it cover every current worker?
• For three workers — can you show me the actual RTW evidence, and confirm it meets Home Office requirements?
• For workers with time-limited permissions — how do you track expiry dates and ensure follow-up checks?
• Where is RTW evidence stored — who has access, and how is it secured?
• How quickly can you produce RTW evidence for an inspection — demonstrate retrieval now.
**What You Are Looking For:**
• RTW check completed for 100% of current workforce before work commenced
• Evidence meets Home Office requirements — correct document type, certified copy, dated
• Time-limited permissions identified and expiry dates actively monitored with follow-up checks in the diary
• Storage is secure — locked filing, access-controlled system, or encrypted digital storage — not open-access shared drives
**Sampling:**
Full population check against a complete workforce list. Sample five for document quality.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• RTW checks completed for most workers but gaps exist with no explanation
• Checks conducted but documents accepted do not meet Home Office requirements
• Time-limited permissions not tracked — expiry dates unknown
• Evidence stored in open-access locations or cannot be retrieved quickly
## **A4 — Payroll Statutory Compliance and Reconciliation**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business is meeting its statutory payroll obligations — National Minimum Wage, Statutory Sick Pay, workplace pension auto-enrolment, and PAYE — and whether payroll calculations are independently reviewed before payment.
**Why This Matters:**
HMRC payroll non-compliance generates penalties, interest, and reputational damage. NMW underpayment attracts 200% penalties and public naming under the Fair Work Agency regime from April 2026, with six-year back-pay recovery powers. SSP non-compliance is now within FWA enforcement scope. Auto-enrolment failures attract TPR fines. A single payroll run with systemic errors creates a compounding liability that grows with every pay period it goes uncorrected.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you walk me through your payroll process — who calculates it, who approves it before payment, and can you show me that NMW, SSP, and pension obligations are being met correctly across your workforce?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Who runs payroll — internal or external? Who reviews it before it is paid?
• Show me a recent payroll summary — can you demonstrate NMW compliance for your lowest-paid workers?
• How do you track and pay SSP — show me a recent example where an employee was off sick.
• Show me your pension provider confirmation of contributions — are all eligible workers enrolled?
• Has HMRC raised any queries or compliance checks in the last three years?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Clear segregation between payroll preparation and payment authorisation
• Documented NMW compliance check — particularly for workers on variable hours or piece rates
• SSP tracked and paid accurately with a clear process for eligibility assessment
• Auto-enrolment obligations met — eligible workers enrolled, contributions made, re-enrolment completed on schedule
• PAYE submissions current with no outstanding HMRC correspondence
**Sampling:**
Last three payroll runs. One SSP case if available. Pension contribution confirmation.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Payroll prepared and approved by the same person with no independent review
• NMW compliance assumed but not calculated — particularly risk with salaried workers on long hours
• SSP managed informally — no documented eligibility assessment or payment tracking
• Auto-enrolment not completed for all eligible workers — opt-outs not properly documented
## **A5 — Holiday Pay Calculation and Entitlement Accuracy**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business is calculating holiday pay correctly — including the required inclusion of regular overtime and commission — and whether six-year employment records are being maintained as required.
**Why This Matters:**
Systematic holiday pay undercalculation creates a compounding liability across the entire workforce over time. The Fair Work Agency has six-year back-pay recovery powers from April 2026. A business that has been paying basic salary only on holiday — excluding regular overtime and commission as required by case law — carries a quantifiable collective liability that becomes a due diligence finding in any transaction. Failure to maintain records for six years is now a criminal offence.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me how holiday pay is calculated for your workforce — particularly for anyone who earns commission or works regular overtime — and can you demonstrate that employment records are being kept for the required six-year period?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Walk me through how holiday pay is calculated — what figure goes into the holiday pay calculation?
• For a salesperson or someone with variable pay — show me how their holiday pay is calculated.
• How far back do your employment records go — can you produce records for a leaver from four years ago?
• Who is responsible for ensuring holiday entitlement is correctly calculated and tracked?
• Has any worker raised a concern or claim about holiday pay in the last three years?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Holiday pay calculations include regular overtime and commission where applicable — not just basic salary
• Entitlement tracked accurately for all workers including part-time and variable-hours staff
• Employment records retained for a minimum of six years
• A clear process for holiday approval and tracking that produces an auditable record
**Sampling:**
Last three months of holiday pay calculations. Records retrieval test for a leaver from three or more years ago.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Holiday pay calculated on basic salary only with no consideration of overtime or commission
• Holiday records maintained for current staff only — no historical retention
• Entitlement tracked informally in spreadsheets with no version control or audit trail
• Variable-hours workers’ entitlement calculated incorrectly or not tracked at all
## **A6 — Employment and People-Risk Policy Framework**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has a minimum set of employment policies that are current, accessible to all workers, and sufficient to provide a credible defence in tribunal proceedings.
**Why This Matters:**
Absent or out-of-date policies are regularly cited in tribunal judgments as evidence of systemic failure. They remove the business’s ability to rely on statutory defences. From April 2026, SSP policy compliance falls within Fair Work Agency enforcement scope. The Employment Rights Act 2025 changes — including day-one unfair dismissal rights from July 2026 — make policy infrastructure materially more important for businesses at early hiring stage.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me your employment policies — in particular your disciplinary and grievance procedure, equal opportunities policy, health and safety policy, data protection policy, and whistleblowing policy — and confirm when they were last reviewed?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me where policies are stored and how workers access them.
• When were these policies last reviewed — is there a review date on each document?
• Do they reflect current law — for example, do they reference the Fair Work Agency or the Employment Rights Act 2025?
• Has the business ever relied on a policy in a disciplinary or grievance situation — what happened?
• Is there an SSP policy, and does it set out how SSP is administered?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• As a minimum: disciplinary and grievance procedure, equal opportunities, health and safety, data protection, whistleblowing, and SSP policy
• Policies reviewed within the last 12 months or following material legislative change
• Accessible to all workers — not locked in a filing cabinet or HR system workers cannot access
• Policies reflect current law including FWA and ERA 2025 developments where relevant
**Sampling:**
Document review of all policies. Spot-check one worker — can they locate the grievance procedure without help?
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Core policies exist but have not been reviewed since before 2024
• Policies exist on paper but workers are unaware of them or cannot access them
• Disciplinary procedure exists but does not reflect the ACAS Code of Practice
• No SSP policy — or SSP handled entirely informally with no documented process
## **A7 — Disputes, Claims, and Case File Governance**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business maintains a register of active and historic employment disputes, and whether case files contain sufficient evidence to defend those matters in tribunal or under FWA investigation.
**Why This Matters:**
The Fair Work Agency from April 2026 can pursue employer-side records going back six years and bring claims on behalf of workers. A business without a disputes register cannot demonstrate visibility of its own risk exposure. Poorly maintained case files — missing investigation records, undocumented outcome letters, no evidence of fair process — convert manageable disputes into indefensible tribunal claims.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me a register of any current or recent employment disputes, grievances, or tribunal claims — and for any that have occurred in the last two years, can you show me the case file?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Do you have a disputes or claims register — show it to me.
• How many grievances, disciplinaries, or tribunal claims have you had in the last three years?
• For a specific case — show me the case file. Does it contain the complaint, the investigation notes, the outcome letter, and evidence that the process was followed?
• Have any settlement agreements been reached — are these on file with evidence of independent legal advice?
• Are there any matters currently outstanding — at ACAS, ET1 stage, or under internal process?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A disputes register that captures all grievances, disciplinaries, capability cases, and tribunal matters
• Case files that contain: the original complaint, investigation notes, right-to-be-accompanied evidence, outcome letter, and appeal outcome if applicable
• Any settlement agreements accompanied by evidence of independent legal advice to the employee
• No outstanding matters that are not actively managed and properly documented
**Sampling:**
Disputes register review. Two case files if available — one closed, one active.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Disputes handled but not logged — informal resolution with no record
• Case files incomplete — outcome letters exist but no investigation notes or fair process evidence
• Settlement agreements on file but no evidence the employee received independent legal advice
• Outstanding matters not tracked — status unknown
## **A8 — Exit and Settlement Governance**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether employment exits — including resignations, dismissals, and mutual terminations — are documented correctly, and whether any settlement agreements are legally valid.
**Why This Matters:**
Poorly documented exits create tribunal risk that does not expire when the employee leaves. A dismissal without documented fair process is an unfair dismissal claim waiting to happen. A settlement agreement without a qualifying independent adviser certificate is not legally binding. An investor conducting pre-transaction due diligence will examine recent exits as a leading indicator of employment practice quality.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_For employees who have left the business in the last two years, can you show me how those exits were managed — and if any settlement agreements were used, can you show me the signed agreement and the independent legal advice certificate?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• How many people have left the business in the last two years — resignations, dismissals, and mutual?
• For a dismissal — show me the process that was followed. Is it documented?
• Are there any settlement agreements — show me the agreement and the COT3 or compromise certificate.
• How were exit interviews handled — is there a record?
• Has any ex-employee made a post-exit claim — at ACAS or tribunal?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Exit process documented for all departures — whether resignation, dismissal, or mutual termination
• Dismissals supported by documented fair process — investigation, hearing, outcome, right of appeal
• Any settlement agreements accompanied by a valid independent legal advice certificate
• Post-exit claims tracked and managed — not absorbed informally
**Sampling:**
All exits in the last 24 months. Any settlement agreements on file.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Exits recorded administratively but no documented fair process for dismissals
• Settlement agreements on file but no independent legal advice certificate — agreement is unenforceable
• Exits managed verbally with no written record of reason or process
• Post-exit claims not tracked — discovery only when correspondence arrives
## **A9 — Whistleblowing and Speak-Up Mechanism Effectiveness**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has a functional whistleblowing mechanism that workers know about, can use without fear of reprisal, and that routes disclosures to someone with appropriate authority.
**Why This Matters:**
A non-functional whistleblowing mechanism fails to surface internal problems early and increases the likelihood that workers escalate concerns externally — to the FWA, the ICO, or the press. The Employment Rights Act 2025 strengthens whistleblower protections significantly. An investor who acquires a business and later discovers suppressed concerns about pay, safety, or conduct that were never properly investigated has inherited a significant governance failure.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me your whistleblowing policy — and can you demonstrate that workers know about it and that any disclosures made through it have been properly handled?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me the whistleblowing policy — where is it and how do workers access it?
• Who does a disclosure go to — and what happens if the concern is about that person?
• Has the mechanism ever been used — if so, how was the disclosure handled?
• Is there any evidence that a worker has raised a concern informally that should have gone through the whistleblowing process?
• When was the policy last reviewed — does it reflect current protected disclosure categories?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A written whistleblowing policy that identifies a named recipient and an alternative route if the concern involves that person
• Workers aware of the policy — communicated at induction and accessible at all times
• Any disclosures handled in a documented, confidential, and protected manner
• Policy reviewed within the last 12 months and reflecting current protected disclosure law
**Sampling:**
Policy review. Worker awareness spot-check if practicable.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Policy exists but routes all disclosures to the MD or founder — no independent alternative route
• Policy exists but workers are unaware of it and cannot locate it
• Disclosures handled informally with no documented process or outcome
• Policy not reviewed since before the Employment Rights Act 2025
## **A10 — Intellectual Property Assignment and Protection**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether IP created by employees, contractors, and founders in the course of their work is legally assigned to the business — and whether the business has taken basic protective steps to register or document its core IP.
**Why This Matters:**
For a technology, product, or content-led business, IP is frequently the primary asset. If contractor agreements do not contain explicit IP assignment clauses, the contractor — not the business — owns what they created. An investor who discovers post-completion that the product was built by contractors who retain ownership of their output has a materially different asset to what was represented.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me that your employment contracts and contractor agreements contain IP assignment clauses — and can you demonstrate that any IP the business considers proprietary has been properly assigned to the company?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me a standard employment contract — does it contain an IP assignment clause?
• Show me a standard contractor agreement — does it explicitly assign IP created under that engagement to the business?
• For any work created by a founder before the company was incorporated — has that IP been formally assigned to the company?
• Has the business registered any trademarks, patents, or designs — show me the registration certificates.
• Is there any IP the business relies on that was created by someone no longer with the business?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Employment contracts contain clear IP assignment clauses covering work created in the course of employment
• Contractor agreements explicitly assign all work product to the business — not relying on implied assignment
• Pre-incorporation IP formally assigned to the company by way of a written assignment deed
• Core brand assets, product names, or patentable inventions registered or protected appropriately
**Sampling:**
Employment contract IP clause review. Contractor agreement IP clause review. Any IP registration documents.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Employment contracts contain IP clauses but contractor agreements are silent on IP ownership
• Pre-incorporation IP relied upon but never formally assigned — founder still holds it personally
• Core IP not registered — no trademark, patent, or design protection despite commercial reliance
• Contracts contain IP clauses but scope is unclear
# **SECTION B — OPERATIONAL GOVERNANCE**
_Controls B1–B13 | 13 controls_
## **B1 — Organisational Structure Currency and Role Clarity**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has a documented and current organisational structure that accurately reflects actual reporting lines, role responsibilities, and spans of control.
**Why This Matters:**
An outdated or absent org structure signals that the business has grown reactively without governance. It creates ambiguity about accountability, makes succession planning impossible, and prevents any objective assessment of management load or key person dependency. For an investor, a business that cannot produce a current and accurate org chart has not been managed — it has been improvised.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me your current organisational chart — and can you confirm that it accurately reflects who reports to whom today, including any recent changes?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me the org chart — when was it last updated?
• Have there been any structural changes in the last six months that are not reflected here?
• Are all roles on the chart filled — or are there vacancies? How long have they been open?
• Who is responsible for maintaining the org chart?
• Does each role have a defined job description or role profile?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A documented org chart that is current — updated within the last three months or following any structural change
• All roles clearly attributed with named individuals
• Reporting lines that reflect actual practice — not historical structure
• Vacancies identified and actively managed
**Sampling:**
Org chart review. Spot-check two roles — does the person know their reporting line?
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Org chart exists but has not been updated following recent changes
• Informal reporting lines in practice that differ from the documented structure
• Roles exist on the chart without named individuals and with no active recruitment
• No individual responsible for maintaining the structure
## **B2 — Authority Framework for People Decisions**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether there is a documented framework setting out who has authority to make specific people decisions — dismissal, pay changes, promotion, disciplinary sanctions — and whether that framework is followed in practice.
**Why This Matters:**
Undocumented or unclear authority for people decisions creates two simultaneous risks: managers making consequential decisions they are not authorised or equipped to make, and the business being bound by commitments made without proper oversight. Pay commitments made by line managers outside any authority framework, or dismissals initiated without HR or director involvement, are a material governance failure that increases tribunal exposure and payroll cost unpredictability.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me how people decisions are made — specifically who has authority to dismiss, change pay, promote, or apply disciplinary sanctions — and can you show me that this is documented and followed?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Who can make the decision to dismiss someone — does that require director involvement?
• If a line manager wants to give someone a pay rise, what process do they follow?
• Has a people decision ever been made by someone who did not have authority to make it — what happened?
• Show me where the authority framework is documented.
• For a recent promotion or pay change — show me the approval trail.
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A documented authority framework specifying who can make each category of people decision
• Consistent application in practice — decisions traceable to the authority holder
• Clear escalation route for decisions that require director or board-level sign-off
• People decisions that create financial commitment reviewed before implementation
**Sampling:**
Authority framework document. Two recent people decisions — trace the approval.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Authority understood informally but not documented
• Pay changes made by line managers without documented approval
• Dismissals initiated without director involvement where required
• Authority framework exists but is not followed consistently
## **B3 — Concentration of Operational and Spend Authority**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether operational decisions and financial commitments above defined thresholds require more than one authorised signatory — and whether the business has documented spending limits by role.
**Why This Matters:**
Concentration of financial authority in a single individual creates two risks: the business cannot function if that individual is unavailable, and there is no check on financial commitments. A sole director who can commit the business to any contract, hire anyone at any salary, and authorise any payment without a second review is a governance control gap that covenant monitoring and credit risk frameworks are specifically designed to detect.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me your spending authority limits — what a role can commit to without a second signatory — and can you demonstrate that this is being followed in practice?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• What is the limit above which a purchase or commitment requires a second authorisation?
• Show me a recent contract commitment above that threshold — how was it approved?
• Can any single individual commit the business to a contract of any value alone?
• Is payroll authorised by someone independent of whoever prepared it?
• Has any commitment been made outside the authority framework — what happened?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Documented financial authority limits by role
• Thresholds that are realistic and followed — not set so high that dual authorisation never applies
• Payroll preparation and authorisation separated between different individuals
• Evidence of dual authorisation for significant recent commitments
**Sampling:**
Spending authority policy. Two significant recent financial commitments — trace the approval.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Authority framework exists on paper but the founder countersigns everything regardless of amount
• No documented thresholds — authority determined by whoever happens to be available
• Payroll prepared and authorised by the same person with no independent review
• Commitments made verbally and ratified retrospectively
## **B4 — Operational Continuity Under Manager Absence**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business can maintain normal operations if any single manager — below founder level — is unavailable for two weeks or more, and whether deputy or cover arrangements are documented.
**Why This Matters:**
Management absence is not a theoretical risk — it is a certainty at some point. A business with no documented cover arrangements will experience delivery disruption, decision-making paralysis, and client-facing failure when it occurs. For a lender assessing covenant compliance or an investor assessing operational resilience, undocumented management continuity signals that the business has not grown its infrastructure at the same pace as its headcount.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_For each of your management roles, can you show me who provides cover if that person is absent for two weeks — and can you demonstrate that cover person has enough knowledge and authority to keep things running?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• If your operations manager was unavailable tomorrow for two weeks — who steps in and what can they do?
• Are cover arrangements documented — or does everyone just know informally?
• Has cover been needed recently — how did it work in practice?
• Does the cover person have access to the systems, contacts, and information they would need?
• Are critical processes documented in a way that someone else could follow them?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Named cover arrangements for each management role — documented and communicated
• Cover persons with sufficient access, authority, and knowledge to maintain operations
• Critical processes documented in a way that is not entirely in the absent manager’s head
• Cover arrangements tested through planned absences or handovers
**Sampling:**
Management roles list. Cover arrangement documentation. One recent absence — how was it handled?
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Cover arrangements understood informally but not documented
• Cover person identified but without access to systems or financial authorities needed
• Critical processes undocumented — operations depend on the absent person’s personal knowledge
• No cover arrangements at all for one or more management roles
## **B5 — Leadership Succession and Founder Dependency Mitigation**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has taken documented steps to reduce its dependency on the founder or a small number of senior individuals — and whether there is a credible succession plan for key leadership roles.
**Why This Matters:**
Founder dependency is the single most commonly cited operational risk in growth business lending and investment decisions. A business where the founder holds all key client relationships, all institutional knowledge, and all decision-making authority is not investable at scale. The question is not whether dependency exists — it almost always does — but whether it is being actively mitigated.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_If you stepped away from the business for six months, what would happen — and can you show me the steps you have taken to make the business less dependent on you personally?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Which client relationships are held personally by you or one or two senior individuals?
• What decisions can only be made by you — and which have you delegated with documented authority?
• Is there a second person who could represent the business credibly to investors, lenders, or major clients?
• What have you done in the last 12 months to reduce founder dependency — show me something concrete.
• If you were incapacitated — who has access to your accounts, passwords, and key contacts?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A documented assessment of where founder or key-person dependency currently sits
• Concrete mitigation steps taken — delegation of decisions, client relationship transfer, documented processes
• A named individual who can credibly act in a senior capacity in the founder’s absence
• Access to critical accounts, systems, and contacts not solely held by one individual
**Sampling:**
Founder interview. Client relationship mapping. Authority delegation documentation.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• All key relationships, decisions, and knowledge concentrated in the founder with no mitigation steps
• Steps described verbally but nothing documented or delegated in practice
• Second-in-command exists but lacks authority, access, or external credibility
• Business continuity entirely dependent on the founder’s personal availability
## **B6 — Onboarding Controls and System Access Management**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether new starters complete a structured onboarding process before accessing systems or client data — including right-to-work verification, contract signing, mandatory briefings, and access provisioning that matches their role.
**Why This Matters:**
Uncontrolled onboarding creates the conditions for right-to-work failures, data protection breaches, and over-privileged system access. An employee who joins and is given unrestricted system access before completing compliance checks, signing their contract, or receiving a data protection briefing has created a compliance gap from day one. Over-provisioned access is a GDPR and cyber risk that scales with headcount.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you walk me through what happens when a new employee or contractor joins — specifically what checks are completed, what they sign, what briefings they receive, and how their system access is provisioned?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me the onboarding checklist or process document.
• At what point is system access granted — before or after right-to-work is verified and contract is signed?
• What data protection or security briefing do new starters receive — when and how?
• How is system access scoped to the role — or does everyone get the same access?
• For a recent starter — show me their completed onboarding record.
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A documented onboarding checklist covering RTW, contract signing, data protection briefing, and access provisioning
• System access granted only after compliance checks are complete
• Access provisioned to match the role — not blanket access to all systems
• Completed onboarding records maintained for each starter
**Sampling:**
Onboarding checklist. Two recent starters — review their onboarding records.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Onboarding process exists informally but is not documented or consistently applied
• System access granted before right-to-work or contract is complete
• All new starters given the same level of system access regardless of role
• No data protection briefing at onboarding
## **B7 — Offboarding and Access Termination**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has a documented offboarding process that ensures system access is revoked, company property is returned, and confidential information obligations are enforced on departure.
**Why This Matters:**
Incomplete offboarding leaves former employees with active access to company systems, client data, and intellectual property. This is both a GDPR failure and a commercial risk if a departing employee downloads client lists or strategic documents before leaving. For businesses that have experienced contentious exits, the absence of a documented offboarding process makes it impossible to demonstrate that access was properly controlled.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you walk me through what happens when an employee or contractor leaves — specifically how system access is revoked, how company property is returned, and how you ensure confidential information obligations are enforced?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me your offboarding checklist or process.
• How quickly is system access revoked when someone leaves — same day, next day, or longer?
• For a recent leaver — can you show me evidence that their access was revoked and equipment returned?
• What happens to their email account and files after they leave?
• Have you ever had a situation where a former employee retained access after leaving — what happened?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A documented offboarding checklist covering access revocation, device return, email and data handling, and confidentiality reminder
• System access revoked on or before the last working day
• Completed offboarding records for recent leavers
• Clear process for handling the former employee’s files and data in compliance with GDPR retention obligations
**Sampling:**
Offboarding checklist. Two recent leavers — confirm access was revoked and when.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Offboarding handled informally — no checklist, no documented completion
• Access revoked days or weeks after departure, or only when remembered
• No documentation of equipment return or data handover
• Former employees’ email accounts left active for extended periods
## **B8 — Payroll and Expense Approval Segregation**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether there is documented segregation between the preparation of payroll and the authorisation of payment — and whether expense claims are approved by someone other than the claimant.
**Why This Matters:**
Payroll fraud and expense abuse are most common in businesses where the same person can prepare, approve, and pay. This is the most frequent internal fraud vector in small businesses. A founder or finance manager who can add a person to payroll, set their salary, and authorise payment without a second review is operating without a basic internal control. For any lender or investor, the absence of segregation at payroll level is a red flag on financial control quality.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me how payroll is prepared, reviewed, and authorised before payment — and can you confirm that the person preparing it is not the same person authorising it?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Who prepares payroll — and who authorises it before it is paid?
• Can those two functions be performed by the same person?
• Show me a payroll approval record from the last pay cycle.
• How are expense claims submitted and approved — can someone approve their own expenses?
• Has any error or discrepancy in payroll or expenses been identified through the review process?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Documented separation between payroll preparation and payment authorisation
• Expense claims approved by a line manager or director who is not the claimant
• Evidence of the approval process in recent payroll and expense records
• Any errors identified through the review process — evidence that the control is working
**Sampling:**
Last three payroll cycles — evidence of preparation and separate authorisation. Sample expense claims.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Payroll prepared and authorised by the same person with no secondary review
• Approval described as happening informally — no documented record
• Expense claims self-approved or approved by a direct report of the claimant
• Segregation exists on paper but is routinely bypassed
## **B9 — Formal Risk Register Existence and Maintenance**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business maintains a formal risk register that captures its material operational risks — and whether that register is updated regularly and owned by someone accountable for its maintenance.
**Why This Matters:**
A business without a risk register is managing risk reactively. For a lender monitoring covenant compliance or an investor assessing governance maturity, the absence of a risk register signals the business is not being managed at the level its size and complexity require. A risk register that exists but has not been updated in 12 months is, operationally, the same as no risk register.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me your risk register — and can you demonstrate that it has been reviewed and updated within the last quarter?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me the risk register.
• When was it last updated — who made those updates?
• Who owns the risk register — is there a named individual responsible for keeping it current?
• Are employment and people risks included in the register?
• Show me the three risks currently rated as highest — what is being done about them?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A formal risk register covering operational, financial, people, and compliance risks
• Updated within the last quarter — with evidence of who made the updates and when
• A named risk owner for each entry and an action owner for each mitigation
• People and employment risks explicitly included
**Sampling:**
Risk register review. Evidence of most recent update.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Risk register exists but has not been updated in 6–12 months
• Register maintained by a single individual with no oversight or review
• People and employment risks absent from the register
• Register exists but actions against risks have no owners or deadlines
## **B10 — Coverage of People and Execution Risks in the Register**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the risk register specifically covers the people and execution risks most likely to threaten operational delivery — and whether entries are sufficiently specific to be actionable.
**Why This Matters:**
A risk register entry that says “key person dependency — medium” without naming the dependency, quantifying the exposure, or identifying mitigation steps is not a risk management tool — it is a compliance artefact. An investor wants to see that the business has thought specifically about what would happen if its top three people left, if its main delivery system failed, or if it lost its largest client.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Looking at the people and execution risks in your register — can you show me that each one is specific enough to be managed, with a named owner and a documented mitigation or action?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me the people and execution risk entries — are they specific or generic?
• For the most significant people risk — what is the mitigation, who owns it, and what has been done?
• Is key person dependency named specifically — which individuals, what is the impact if they leave?
• Are delivery risks captured — client concentration, single-supplier dependency, key system failure?
• When a risk entry is actioned and closed — how is that recorded?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• People risks named specifically — not “key person dependency” generically but which person, what knowledge, what impact
• Each risk entry has a named owner, a risk rating, and a documented mitigation or action
• Delivery and execution risks included — not just employment and compliance risks
• Evidence that mitigations are being implemented — not just recorded
**Sampling:**
People and execution risk entries in the register. Evidence of action against the top three.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• People risks recorded generically without naming individuals or quantifying impact
• Risk entries have no named owners or documented mitigations
• Delivery risks absent — register focused exclusively on compliance
• Mitigations described but no evidence of implementation
## **B11 — Risk Governance in Operation and Action Discipline**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the risk register is actively reviewed at board or management level on a regular schedule — and whether actions identified to mitigate risks are being tracked to completion.
**Why This Matters:**
A risk register that is not reviewed is a document, not a control. The test of whether risk governance is operational is whether the board or management team can demonstrate that they discuss risks regularly, that actions are tracked and completed, and that the register evolves as the risk profile changes. An investor wants evidence of a closed loop — risk identified, action set, action completed, risk reassessed.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me that the risk register is reviewed at board or management level regularly — and can you show me that actions identified to manage risks have been followed through to completion?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me board or management meeting minutes where risks were reviewed in the last quarter.
• For an action that appeared in the register three months ago — has it been completed?
• How often is the risk register formally reviewed — is it on the board agenda?
• Who is responsible for ensuring risk actions are completed — and what happens if they are not?
• Has the risk register changed in the last six months in response to new risks emerging?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Evidence of regular risk register review — at least quarterly, documented in board or management minutes
• Actions tracked to completion — not just logged and forgotten
• Risk ratings updated as mitigations are implemented
• Emerging risks added to the register in response to changes in the business or external environment
**Sampling:**
Board or management meeting minutes — last two reviews. Open actions from prior reviews — status check.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Risk register reviewed once a year at most
• Actions logged but not tracked — same actions appearing in successive reviews without progress
• Risk register not on the board agenda
• No evidence of risk ratings being updated following implementation of mitigations
## **B12 — Data Protection and GDPR Compliance**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has the foundational GDPR compliance infrastructure in place — including a Record of Processing Activities, a privacy notice, a data breach response process, and a data retention policy.
**Why This Matters:**
ICO enforcement has moved beyond large organisations. Growth businesses holding employee, customer, and contractor personal data are within scope of GDPR obligations from the moment they process a single person’s data. A data subject access request that cannot be fulfilled, a breach not reported within 72 hours, or a privacy notice that does not reflect actual processing are each individual compliance failures. An investor acquiring a business with no GDPR infrastructure is acquiring an unquantified ICO exposure.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you show me your GDPR compliance documentation — specifically your Record of Processing Activities, your privacy notice, your breach response process, and your data retention policy?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me your Record of Processing Activities — does it cover employee, customer, and contractor data?
• Where is your privacy notice — does it reflect what you actually do with personal data?
• If a data breach occurred today — what would you do in the first 72 hours?
• How long do you retain employee personal data after they leave?
• Has the business received any subject access requests — how were they handled?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A documented ROPA covering all categories of personal data processed by the business
• A current privacy notice accessible to employees, customers, and contractors
• A documented breach response process
• A data retention policy that specifies how long different categories of data are kept and how they are disposed of
**Sampling:**
ROPA review. Privacy notice review. Breach process test — walk through a scenario.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• ROPA exists but has not been updated to reflect current data processing activities
• Privacy notice not reviewed since GDPR came into force
• No documented breach response process — response would be improvised
• Data retention policy absent — personal data retained indefinitely or deleted arbitrarily
## **B13 — Information Security and Cyber Risk Controls**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business has foundational information security controls in place — including access control, backup and recovery, device management, and cyber incident response — appropriate to its size and the sensitivity of the data it holds.
**Why This Matters:**
Cyber incidents are the most frequently occurring operational disruption for SMEs. A business that cannot recover from a ransomware attack within days, that has never tested its backup, or that has no process for responding to a breach is carrying an unquantified business continuity risk. For a lender, an unrecoverable cyber incident affects covenant compliance. For an investor, it affects enterprise value.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_Can you walk me through your information security arrangements — specifically how access to systems is controlled, how data is backed up and recovered, and what your response would be if you suffered a cyber incident today?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• How is access to company systems controlled — who decides who gets access to what?
• When did you last test your backup — can you demonstrate that recovery works?
• What would happen if you were hit by ransomware today — who would you call, what would you do?
• Are personal devices used for work — if so, what controls are in place?
• Has the business ever suffered a cyber incident or data loss — what happened?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• Access control appropriate to the business — not everyone has admin rights to everything
• Regular automated backups with a tested recovery process
• A documented cyber incident response process
• Device management policy covering personal and company devices used for work
**Sampling:**
Backup schedule and last test date. Access control review. Incident response document.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Backups exist but have never been tested — recovery capability unconfirmed
• All users have admin rights — no access control proportionate to role
• No documented incident response process
• Personal devices used for work with no policy or security requirements applied

## **C1 — People Risk Leading Indicators and Early-Warning Capability**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business tracks defined people and operational metrics on a regular basis — and whether those metrics are reviewed at management level in a way that enables early intervention before risks materialise.
**Why This Matters:**
Absence of early-warning indicators means operational and people risks are only identified after they have already caused damage. Growth-stage business failures are rarely sudden — they are preceded by rising absence, increasing turnover, declining delivery performance, and capacity strain that went unmeasured. An investor wants evidence that the business is managing forward, not backward.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_What people and operational metrics do you track regularly — and can you show me the most recent management review where those metrics were discussed and acted upon?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me your people metrics dashboard or MI pack — what does it cover?
• What is your current employee turnover rate — and is that higher or lower than six months ago?
• What is your current absence rate — and how does it compare to the same period last year?
• Show me a recent management meeting where these metrics were reviewed — what decisions were made?
• If turnover or absence spiked next month, at what point would it trigger a formal management response?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A defined set of people and operational metrics tracked at least monthly
• Metrics reviewed at management level with evidence of discussion and action where indicators deteriorate
• Metrics that cover at minimum: turnover, absence, time-to-hire, and delivery capacity
• An agreed threshold at which deteriorating metrics generate a formal management response
**Sampling:**
Last two management information packs or reporting cycles. Evidence of review and resulting actions.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Metrics tracked informally — calculated when asked but not reported routinely
• Metrics reported but not reviewed — appearing in packs that no one reads
• No defined trigger points — management response entirely at discretion
## **C2 — Delivery Disruption Logging and Organisational Learning**
**What You Are Testing:**
Whether the business logs delivery disruptions, operational failures, and near-misses caused by people or management issues — and whether root cause analysis and preventive action are documented.
**Why This Matters:**
Operational failures that are not recorded are repeated. A business that absorbs delivery disruptions silently — managing the immediate fallout without logging, analysing, or learning from them — will encounter the same failures again. Lenders assessing operational resilience and investors assessing management quality both look for evidence that the business treats operational failures as information, not embarrassment.
**Primary Question (Ask Verbatim):**
_When delivery disruptions or operational failures occur — particularly those driven by people or management issues — can you show me how they are logged, what analysis is done, and what actions are taken to prevent recurrence?_
**Follow-Up Evidence Prompts (What You Want Them to Show You):**
• Show me your disruption log, incident register, or RAID log — does it capture people-driven issues?
• For a specific recent disruption — show me the entry, the root cause analysis, and the action taken.
• How are near-misses captured — or only actual failures?
• Who reviews the log — and how often?
• Can you point to a process change that resulted from a logged disruption?
**What You Are Looking For:**
• A disruption or incident log that captures people-driven failures
• Root cause analysis documented for significant disruptions
• Actions tracked to completion with named owners and deadlines
• Evidence of organisational learning — a process or control that changed as a result of a logged failure
**Sampling:**
Disruption or incident log review. One significant recent disruption — trace from log entry to action completion.
**If Evidence Is Partial:**
• Disruptions handled reactively and informally with no log maintained
• Log exists but contains descriptions only — no root cause or actions
• Actions logged but not tracked or completed — same issues recurring
• Near-misses not captured — only actual failures that could not be ignored
